## How To Play The Deep Sea

You start bobbing at the surface next to a little sailboat. Scroll down inside the game box to sink. On a phone, swipe up with your finger. On a computer, you can also click the ocean and use the arrow keys, Page Down or the space bar.

The meter in the top corner shows how deep you are in meters and feet, plus the name of the zone you are in. The little yellow arrow on the left edge marks the exact depth the meter is reading.

Want to skip ahead? Press **Dive faster** to jump to the next stop, or **Back up** to rise to the one before. Every stop has a card with a name, its depth and one fact. Keep going until you hit the very bottom.

## What Your Score Means

Your score is the deepest point you reach. Here is what the numbers mean as you sink:

- **0 to 200 m, the sunlight zone:** bright water, coral reefs, turtles and divers.
- **200 to 1,000 m, the twilight zone:** too dim for plants. Fish start making their own light.
- **1,000 to 4,000 m, the midnight zone:** no sunlight at all. Whales dive here to hunt squid.
- **4,000 to 6,000 m, the abyss:** cold, dark plains where sea pigs stroll across the mud.
- **Below 6,000 m, the hadal zone:** deep trenches with crushing pressure and very few visitors.

Reach 10,935 meters and you have touched Challenger Deep, the deepest known spot on Earth.

## Tips To Improve

- **Use the Dive faster button** when you want to jump from stop to stop instead of scrolling past empty water.
- **Read the cards in the twilight zone.** The strangest animals live between 500 and 2,000 meters.
- **Watch the colors.** The water gets darker the deeper you go, the same way real sunlight fades.
- **Look for things that glow.** Lures, spots and lights shine brighter once the water turns black.

## Fun Facts About the Deep Sea

The ocean is far deeper than it is easy to imagine. Mount Everest is 8,849 meters tall, yet if you dropped it into Challenger Deep, its peak would still sit about 2 kilometers underwater.

Pressure grows fast as you sink. Every 10 meters of water adds about as much pressure as the whole sky pressing down at the surface. At the bottom of the trench, it is over 1,000 times stronger than what you feel right now.

Only a few dozen people have ever visited the very bottom. The first two went down in 1960 in a deep-diving vessel called a bathyscaphe, and almost nobody followed them for over 50 years.
