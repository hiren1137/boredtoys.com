## How To Play the I'm Not a Robot Game

You have probably clicked a little box that says you are not a robot a hundred times. Here you have to do it 23 times in a row, and the website gets more suspicious of you with every step.

Each level shows one check inside a card. Read the blue bar at the top, then do what it says. Sometimes you tick a box. Sometimes you pick picture tiles, type a wobbly word, move a slider or tap a button at just the right moment. When you are ready, press **Verify**. Some levels check your answer the moment you tap.

Pass a check and the next one unlocks. Fail one and it resets with a fresh puzzle, plus a hint under the card. There is no limit on tries, but every failed attempt is counted.

A clock runs at the top while you play. If you need a break, just close the tab. Your level and time are kept in your browser, so a refresh puts you right back where you were.

## What Your Score Means

Your result shows how many levels you cleared, how long it took and how many attempts went wrong.

- **A few levels:** you met the trickier checks and decided you might be a robot after all. That is allowed.
- **Halfway or more:** you can handle disguised robots, colorful words and a shy checkbox. Most of the real nonsense is still ahead.
- **All 23 levels:** you are officially human, at least by our silly standards. Now the goal is speed. Your fastest full clear is saved as your best.

A run with zero failed attempts is very rare. Some levels are designed to catch you the first time.

## Tips To Improve

- **Read every word of the question.** Several levels are traps. A question might ask for something that is not there at all.
- **Do not trust what words say.** When colors are involved, look at the ink and ignore the letters.
- **Count out loud.** For timing and holding levels, saying "one banana, two banana" works better than guessing.
- **Use the hint.** After one miss, the hint usually tells you exactly how the trick works.
- **Learn the order, then race.** The levels always come in the same order, but the pictures, words and numbers change every time. A second run is much faster than the first.

## Why Websites Ask If You Are a Robot

Those checks are called CAPTCHAs. The name stands for "Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart." The term comes from computer scientists at Carnegie Mellon University, including Luis von Ahn and Manuel Blum. They described it in their 2003 paper "CAPTCHA: Using Hard AI Problems for Security."

The idea is simple: give a task that is easy for people and hard for programs, like reading squiggly letters. As computers got better at those tasks, the checks kept changing. Some newer ones barely ask you anything and just look at how you use the page.

Every level here is made up for fun. No real security checks were harmed, and no robots were turned away. Well, maybe one in a mustache.
