## How To Play the Sequence Memory Test

You see a board of nine sticker tiles. Press Start and watch closely. One tile lights up and plays a little note. When it stops, the status line says Your turn. Tap the same tile.

Got it? Great. Now the pattern plays again from the very start, and one new step is added to the end. Level 2 has two flashes, level 3 has three, and so on. You always repeat the whole thing from the first tile, not just the new one.

The first wrong tap ends your run. The tile you pressed turns red, and the one you should have pressed gets a dashed ring so you can see where it went wrong.

You can play with a mouse, by tapping with your finger, or with the number keys 1 to 9. Each tile shows its number in the corner.

## What Your Score Means

Your score is the last level you finished without a mistake. It counts how many steps in a row you could hold in your head and play back.

- **Levels 1 to 4:** you are warming up. Everyone slips on an early level now and then, usually from tapping too fast.
- **Levels 5 to 8:** a solid run. You are keeping a real chain of steps in order.
- **Levels 9 to 12:** strong. Somewhere around here most people need a trick, like chunking or saying the numbers in their head.
- **Level 13 and up:** seriously impressive. You are holding a long chain that grew one step at a time.

Because the same pattern repeats each round, you get to practice it over and over. That is why you can usually go further here than on a test that shows a brand new list every time.

## Tips To Improve

- **Say the numbers.** Whisper "1, 5, 9" as the tiles flash. Hearing the list helps you hold it.
- **Make chunks.** Group the steps into small sets of two or three, like a phone number. "Top row, then middle" is easier than six separate taps.
- **Look for shapes.** Sometimes the path makes a line, a corner or a zigzag. Shapes are easier to remember than single spots.
- **Listen to the notes.** Every tile has its own pitch. After a few rounds the pattern starts to sound like a short tune.
- **Do not rush.** There is no timer on your turn. Take a breath before the first tap.

## The Science of Short-Term Memory

In 1956 the psychologist George Miller wrote a famous paper called "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two." He pointed out that people can usually hold about seven separate items in short-term memory at once. Later research, including work by Nelson Cowan, suggested the true limit may be closer to four chunks, and that grouping is how people stretch it.

Researchers have used a similar idea for decades. The Corsi block-tapping test, created by Philip Corsi in the early 1970s, asks people to tap blocks in the same order a tester did. The electronic toy Simon, released in 1978, turned the same idea into a game with lights and sounds.
