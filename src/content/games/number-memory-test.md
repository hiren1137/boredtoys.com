## How To Play the Number Memory Test

Press Start and a number pops up in big letters. Under it, a purple bar shrinks as your viewing time runs out. When the bar is empty, the number disappears and an answer box takes its place.

Type the number you saw and press Enter or Submit. On a phone, the box brings up your number keypad.

Get it right and you pass the level. The next number is one digit longer. The first round is a single digit, so you have time to warm up. Get even one digit wrong and the run is over.

At the end, the game puts the real number next to what you typed. Digits you missed are marked in red, so you can see exactly where your memory let go.

## What Your Score Means

Your score is the longest number you remembered perfectly. Here is a rough way to read it:

- **1 to 4 digits:** you are just getting started. Most people improve after a couple of runs.
- **5 to 6 digits:** about the length of a short phone number. A good, steady result.
- **7 to 8 digits:** right in the range that memory researchers often talk about.
- **9 to 11 digits:** you are using tricks, whether you know it or not.
- **12 digits or more:** that takes real practice and a solid system.

Psychologists have tested memory like this for over a hundred years with a task called a digit span test. In 1956, psychologist George Miller wrote a famous paper called "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two." It argued that people can hold about seven items in their short-term memory at once. Later research suggests the true limit for separate pieces may be even smaller, and that clever grouping is what lets people go further.

## Tips To Improve

- **Chunk it.** Do not try to hold 9 separate digits. Hold three groups of three, like 482 615 937. Phone numbers are split up for this exact reason.
- **Say it in your head.** Repeat the number silently while the bar runs down. The rhythm of the words helps it stick.
- **Turn digits into things.** 1945 can be a year. 365 is days in a year. 24 is hours in a day. Meaning is easier to remember than random numbers.
- **Look at the whole thing first.** Glance at the full number before you start memorizing, so you know how to split it.
- **Type right away.** The longer you wait, the more the number fades. Start typing as soon as the box appears.

## Why Numbers Slip Away So Fast

Short-term memory is like a tiny whiteboard. It holds a few things for a few seconds, and new things quickly wipe out the old ones. That is why you can forget a code in the time it takes to walk to the next room.

Memory athletes who recite hundreds of digits are not born with a bigger whiteboard. They practice systems that turn digits into pictures, places and stories, then store those in long-term memory instead. With a little practice and some chunking, you can stretch your own score further than you might expect.
